A Budget Location Is Not a Verdict

The scariest number in the AI-and-jobs debate proves less than it looks like. Here is a prompt that makes AI referee the fight instead of joining a side.

By John Andrews·Prompted.daily·3 min read·November 23, 2026

Anthropic is about to go public into a market that has lost its nerve about whether AI can earn back what it costs. The loudest version of that worry ran in the Times this week, and it rests on one number that sounds like a verdict: only fifteen cents of every corporate AI dollar now sits in the IT department. The rest lands in marketing, product, and sales, the departments that hire people. The conclusion drawn is that AI has stopped being a tool and started coming for the wage bill.

Handing that article to a chatbot and asking what it thinks is easy and close to useless. The sharper move is to make the model referee the argument against itself, build the other side's strongest case, and report where it is genuinely unsure. Run that way, it did something the article never does to itself.

The referee's steelman came out stronger than the article's own. The capex math only closes against one line item, because white-collar wages are the only corporate expense large enough to pay a two-trillion-dollar annual bill, and the thesis does not even need layoffs, only a hiring freeze that lets AI absorb the work new people would have done.

Then it turned on that case, and refused to let any counterargument off easy. A tight labor market with a demographic cliff does not refute the thesis, it relocates the pain, because the jobs going unfilled, nursing, trades, logistics, are not the jobs AI targets. New jobs will come, but on a lag measured in decades while displacement runs in years. The strongest counter, the one I leaned on hardest, is that companies are drowning in redundant software, so much of that spend outside IT is eating bloated tool stacks, not people. The model granted the point and took back half of it: consolidation is real but bounded, since the whole software bill is smaller than the wage bill by roughly an order of magnitude, so cleanup buys a couple of calm years and cannot fund the bet.

Here is where the referee earned its keep, by naming the move the article actually makes. Its strongest argument is conditional. If the industry must earn two trillion a year, then the money probably comes partly from labor, because no other pool is large enough. That is a claim about where the money would have to come from, not whether the revenue appears, or when, or how fast the labor share moves. The article slides from "wages are the only line item big enough" to "the labor share collapses in under five years." That slide, from a conditional about magnitude to a dated prediction about timing, is the whole trick.

Fifteen cents in IT is a budget snapshot read as a trend. The confident timeline is a guess wearing the clothes of analysis.

The one thing I would not have reached alone was its answer to what to watch. Not budgets, not layoff announcements. Watch entry-level hiring in exposed white-collar roles, the ratio of junior openings to senior ones. The quiet version of this story shows up first as jobs never posted, not as pink slips.

A summary tells you what someone else concluded. A referee makes you watch the reasoning and shows you where it is thin. What this one exposed is plain: no one knows the timeline, and anyone selling a dated collapse is guessing with a straight face. The direction is plausible, because the money does have to come from somewhere. The five-year verdict is not knowable, and confident prediction here is the tell of weak analysis, not strong. The disciplined reader does not pick a camp. They name the one series that will settle it and watch it move, which is the difference between thinking with these tools and being argued at by a byline.

The prompt

The move is repeatable on any argument that arrives pre-decided. Make the model build the strongest version of a thesis, then the strongest case against it, force it to say for each counter whether it refutes, delays, or merely relocates the claim, and make it name the evidence that would settle the thing. Paste this, and feed it a thesis you suspect is overconfident.

▸ the referee
Act as an economist who referees arguments rather than cheerleads them. I'm going to give you a thesis: that the AI capex boom will pay for itself by drawing down the white-collar wage bill, and that budget data showing AI spend leaking out of IT already proves it.

Steelman that thesis in its strongest form. Then build the strongest counter-case using three forces it underweights: a tight labor market with a demographic cliff, historical new-job creation, and enterprise over-tooling that AI can consolidate. For each force, tell me specifically whether it refutes the thesis, delays it, or just relocates where the pain lands. Rank the three by how much they actually move the conclusion. End by naming the one piece of evidence that would tell me which story is winning over the next 24 months. Do not resolve it into comfort. Tell me where you are genuinely uncertain.

To point it at yourself instead of the news, hand it your situation and ask it to place you, doing work only you can frame or producing output a budget line could re-route. Push on the one claim you find too convenient and make it defend or fold. Then ask for the single piece of evidence that would change its mind this year, and go watch that instead of the headlines.
